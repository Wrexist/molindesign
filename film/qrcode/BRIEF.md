## 1. Topic and one hard idea

**Part of every QR code is backup. At the top level, about 30% of its bytes can be rebuilt, but only if the three corner squares survive, because those tell the camera where the code is and which way is up.**

**The film is a puzzle built on a real object** (hallucinate lesson: a real document or object on frame 0 reads as "not AI"; battery and memory lessons: let the viewer check it themselves). Frame 0 is a real, torn, paper QR code that **we have tested** scans (see §5.1). The question in line 2 ("how does it read what's gone?") is answered by the backup math (Reed–Solomon, the same math Denso says music CDs use), with one real number (level H, about 30%). The turn at 17 s is the honest condition, spoken and on screen: only if the three corner squares survive. Then why corners matter, the DM-able origin story (the inventors searched magazines and boxes for the rarest pattern), 1994 and dirty car-parts factories, and the honest coda: most codes carry less, about 15%. The end sends the viewer to the nearest QR code: "Which corner is empty?" (one-word answer: bottom-right, or "bottom right"), and the last frame cuts back to the torn code.

**Main search phrase: `QR code`** (spoken at 0.2–2.0 s, in the hook). Show it on screen in the first 2 s and in the caption's first line. Secondary phrases for the SEO agent: `how do QR codes work`, `torn QR code still scans`, `damaged QR code`, `QR code error correction`, `why QR codes have three squares`, `QR code corners`. ## 2. Hook options

1. **"This torn QR code still scans."** (chosen, 6 words) It's about one object we show and test on camera, so it can't overclaim: it doesn't say how much, or that any code survives any tear. It carries the search phrase, it's a contradiction the viewer can see (a ripped code, a scan tick), and frame 0 is a real paper prop. The condition is on screen from frame 0 in big type (**LEVEL H · CORNERS INTACT**, ≥ 48 px) and spoken at 17 s.
2. "You can rip a third of this QR code off and it still works." (the sheet hook, rejected) 14 words, more than twice the limit, and it overclaims twice: Denso's "approx. 30%" is **30% of codewords** (8-bit units), not 30% of the printed area, and a third (33%) is over 30%. A tear across a corner square or the format strips kills the scan however small it is, and most everyday codes are level M (about 15%), not H.
3. "Rip it. It still scans." (5 words, backup) Strongest motion on frame 0, but "it" has no object for a muted viewer until the picture explains, and it loses the search phrase in the first 2 s.

**Wording choices tied to sources**
- `hook` claims only that **this** code still scans. The director must make that true by test (§5.1). It doesn't say how much was torn.
- `trick` "part of every code is backup": every level, even L, adds Reed–Solomon codewords (facts 1, 5).
- `thirty` says "**up to** 30% of its **bytes**", at "**level H**". Denso says "approx. 30% of codewords", and a codeword is 8 bits (facts 3, 4). It does **not** say 30% of the area: the printed square also holds the corner squares, timing lines, format strips and quiet margin, and a tear's area doesn't map one-to-one to codewords. Screen: **LEVEL H · ≈30% OF CODEWORDS** and credit **DENSO WAVE · ISO/IEC 18004**.
- `corners` is the condition, spoken and on screen big (Truth lesson: the condition in big type, same frame): **ONLY IF THE CORNERS SURVIVE**.
- `where` paraphrases fact 7–8: the squares say a code is there and where it is.
- `up` "which way is up": Denso says the pattern lets orientation be determined at any of 360° (fact 8). "With three" is ours; Denso puts three at three corners and says three let position be recognized correctly (fact 7).
- `rare` is fact 8 nearly word for word; "inventors" = Hara's two-person team.
- `factory` "Built in 1994": Denso Wave announced it in 1994 (facts 7, 9); development took a year and a half before that, so don't say "invented in 1994" on screen without the DENSO credit (fact 10 says "invented… in 1994"). "Dirty car-parts factories": DENSO is a car-parts maker; the code came from its manufacturing sites and is dirt-resistant "an advantage in manufacturing sites"; Q/H are for factories "where QR Code get dirty" (facts 6, 7, 10). "Track car parts" was avoided: only DENSO's blocked pages say inventory/kanban directly.
- `most` attributes the claim ("its maker says"): Denso says level M is "most frequently selected" (fact 6). It's Denso's statement about what users pick, not a measured count of codes in the wild. Screen: **LEVEL M ≈ 15% · "MOST FREQUENTLY SELECTED" · DENSO WAVE**.
- `end`: the empty corner is bottom-right on a standard (Model 2) QR code held upright (three patterns at three corners, fact 7). Micro QR codes have one square; ignore them.

**Kept out on purpose**
- **"You can rip a third off."** Over 30%, and area ≠ codewords (see hook option 2).
- **Format information and timing patterns by name.** ISO/IEC 18004 puts the format information (error level and mask) in strips beside the three corner squares, stored twice; a scanner needs one readable copy. True, but I couldn't read it in a Denso page or the (paid) standard itself, so it isn't narrated. The director's tear must avoid those strips anyway (§5.1), and "corners intact" covers them on screen.
- **Erasures vs errors.** Reed–Solomon can fix about twice as many missing bytes when it knows where they are as bytes that are silently wrong; Denso's 30% is the errors figure (its worked example: correcting 50 needs 100). Engineering detail, second idea, and most phone decoders don't report which they use. Out.
- **Logos in the middle.** A good DM-able fact, but Denso's own FAQ warns against it (fact 11). Pinned comment, with that caveat, not narration.
- **Kanban / Toyota by name.** Only from DENSO pages I could not open. Description at most, after a browser check.
- **Data capacity numbers** (7,089 digits etc.). A list; off topic.
