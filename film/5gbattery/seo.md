Is 5G draining your battery? It can, mostly when you're barely using it. In a 2021 lab test, 5G was up to 79% less energy-efficient than 4G at slow speeds, and up to 5 times more efficient at full speed. That's why iPhone's 5G Auto and Pixel's Adaptive connectivity drop to LTE when 5G won't help. Those percentages are energy efficiency (energy per bit of data), not battery life.

The test. Researchers at the University of Minnesota and the University of Michigan, "A Variegated Look at 5G in the Wild: Performance, Power, and QoE Implications" (ACM SIGCOMM 2021): "We use Monsoon Power Monitor [17] to power smartphones and measure the power consumption." Their finding: "In terms of energy efficiency for downlink (and uplink), 5G can be 79% (74%) less efficient than 4G at a low throughput but up to 5× (2×) more when the throughput is high." And: "Although at low throughput levels the power consumption for mmWave 5G is higher, it becomes more efficient when the throughput is high." Their own idea: "One solution would be forcing the UE to stay in 4G when high throughput is not needed."

iPhone. Apple Support, "Use 5G with your iPhone": "5G Auto: Enables Smart Data mode. When 5G speeds don’t provide a noticeably better experience, your iPhone automatically switches to LTE, saving battery life." And: "5G On: Always uses 5G network when it’s available. This might reduce battery life." Apple also says: "The default settings for 5G on iPhone optimize battery life and data usage based on your data plan."

Pixel. Google Pixel Phone Help: "Adaptive connectivity turns on 5G only when your app needs increased speed, like when you stream or download videos." And: "Tip: To save battery power, turn on Adaptive connectivity."

How to check yours. iPhone 12 and later: Settings › Cellular › Cellular Data Options › Voice & Data, then see which is picked: 5G Auto, 5G On or LTE. With Dual SIM, pick your number first. Pixel 4a (5G) and later: Settings › Network & internet › Adaptive connectivity. Other Android phones: look for the network mode or preferred network type setting; menu names vary.

Fine print: The numbers are from one 2021 study. Scope as shown in the video: mmWave 5G, one phone (Galaxy S20 Ultra), Verizon, 2021, data coming down to the phone. The paper: "These experiments were done on S20U over Verizon." The percentages are energy efficiency, the energy used per bit of data. They are not battery life: no hours of battery were measured, and 79% less efficient doesn't mean 79% more drain. On that phone, mmWave 5G overtook 4G at about 187 Mbps. Other phones, newer modems, carriers and other 5G bands can differ; the authors note "different UE models may have varied levels of power consumption". "5G costs extra when you barely use it" is our plain-words summary of the paper. Apple's 5G settings need an iPhone 12 or later. Google: "In the United States, some carriers may limit Adaptive connectivity." The chart and screens are drawn and re-created by hand; no real interface or logo is shown.   **Reply:** Could be. In that 2021 test, 5G cost more when little data was moving, and the authors' own idea was keeping the phone on 4G "when high throughput is not needed". - **If:** That study is from 2021, phones are different now.

- **If:** 79% less efficient means 79% more battery drain?
  **Reply:** No. It's energy per bit of data, measured at the phone's power supply in a lab, not hours of battery. Your screen and apps draw on the same battery, so what it means for your day depends on your phone and how you use it.
