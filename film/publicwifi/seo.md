Is public Wi-Fi safe? The FTC's answer: usually, yes. Its consumer advice article "Are Public Wi-Fi Networks Safe? What You Need To Know" (February 2023) says: "In the early days of the internet, they often weren't secure. But things have changed."

Why? "That's because most websites didn't use encryption to scramble the data and protect it from hackers snooping on the network." Now: "Today, most websites do use encryption to protect your information." So the FTC says: "Because of the widespread use of encryption, connecting through a public Wi-Fi network is usually safe."

The catch: scammers "also create fake websites and encrypt them to make you think they're safe when they're not." And: "If you visit a scammer's website, your data may be encrypted on its way to the site, but it won't be safe from scammers operating the site." Encryption protects the trip, not the site.

So check the site's name, letter by letter. Apple's Safari guide: "Some fraudulent websites masquerade as trusted websites by changing one or two letters of the website address." Google's Chrome Help: "Even on secure sites, be careful with your personal information. To make sure you're on the correct site, check the site name in the address bar."

What about the lock? The FTC's 2023 article says: "Look for a lock symbol or https in the address bar to the left of the website address." Browsers have changed since: Google's Chrome Help now calls the secure-site icon "Default (Secure)", and Apple's Safari guide says the words "Not Secure" appear when a site isn't encrypted. Either way, a secure connection doesn't make a site honest, so the name check still matters.

One more from the FTC: "Create and use strong passwords and turn on two-factor authentication when it's available."
