**Description** (paste it in full; the PC can't read repo files). No < or > characters (checked by script):

> One tap. Nine photos. So why do phone photos look better than real life? Because your photo isn't one moment. It's a blend.
>
> It starts shooting before you even tap. Google says that as soon as you open the Pixel camera app, it begins capturing frames into a buffer that keeps erasing the oldest ones; when you press the shutter, it sends the most recent 9 or 15 frames to its HDR+ software. Apple said the same kind of thing about Deep Fusion on the iPhone 11 Pro at its September 10, 2019 event: nine images, most of them shot before you press the button.
>
> Here's the trick. Google's HDR+ captures "a rapid series of deliberately underexposed images". Shooting dark keeps the bright sky from turning white, but dark frames come out grainy. So the phone lines the frames up and averages them. The grain is random and changes every frame; the scene doesn't. Averaging keeps the scene and thins out the grain. Google: "The idea of averaging frames to reduce imaging noise is as old as digital imaging."
>
> At night, a Pixel's Night Sight stacks up to 15 frames handheld (6 longer ones on a tripod). In Google's own words, it takes clean shots "and makes them brighter than reality."
>
> So, was your last photo real? Yes or no.
>
> Fine print: frame counts are what Google said for Pixel phones (2018: 9 or 15 frames on the shutter press, up to 15 for Night Sight handheld; 2021: between 2 and 15 frames depending on conditions) and what Apple said for the iPhone 11 Pro's Deep Fusion in 2019 (nine images). Other phones and newer models may differ, and no single number fits every phone or every shot. "Starts shooting before you tap" is about the everyday photo; Google says Night Sight in dim light waits until after you press the shutter. "Too dark on purpose" describes HDR+'s capture strategy; since 2021 Pixel's HDR+ with Bracketing adds some longer frames, and Deep Fusion adds one long exposure. "The grain mostly cancels" is our plain wording: averaging reduces noise, it doesn't remove it. Apple's nine-image figure comes from its keynote, which we could not re-check word for word, so it is not shown as a quote. The pier, sunset and night scenes are hand-made illustrations, not real photos, and the stacking in the video is a real per-pixel average of nine frames with random grain.
