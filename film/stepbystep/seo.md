Five words: "Let's think step by step." Why does that help AI?

In a 2022 paper from the University of Tokyo and Google, Kojima et al. showed that large language models "are decent zero-shot reasoners by simply adding "Let's think step by step" before each answer." With one 2022 model (InstructGPT, text-davinci-002, a GPT-3 series model), accuracy on the MultiArith math word problems went "from 17.7% to 78.7%".

Try the paper's own puzzle: 16 balls, half are golf balls, half of the golf balls are blue. How many blue golf balls? Asked straight, the model said 8. With the five words, it wrote the steps first (16 balls, 8 golf balls, 4 blue) and then answered 4.

Why it works: a chatbot guesses its answer from the text in front of it. In the paper's method, the steps the model writes are fed back in with the question before the answer is asked for. The paper calls that prompt "self-augmented, since the prompt contains the sentence z generated by the same language model." So the answer is guessed with those steps in view.

Order matters. In a separate 2022 paper, Wei et al. (Google) tested steps written only after the answer. That version "performs about the same as the baseline", meaning no steps at all.

Newer reasoning models do the steps on their own. OpenAI's reasoning guide says: "Since these models perform reasoning internally, prompting them to "think step by step" or "explain your reasoning" is unnecessary." A 2025 Wharton report found that for models built to reason, chain-of-thought prompting "often results in only marginal, if any, gains in answer accuracy."

So, do you still need them? Yes or no.

Fine print: the voice rounds 17.7% and 78.7% to 18 and 79; the screen shows the exact figures. Both numbers are one model (text-davinci-002, run through OpenAI's API in April and May 2022) on one test (MultiArith); the same paper found on-par results on two easier math tests, no gains on its commonsense tests, and that step-by-step reasoning "is not effective" on smaller models.
