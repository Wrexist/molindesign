```
Is this video real? Here's how to tell if a video is AI: the glitches people hunt for are getting harder to spot, so check who posted it first.

The classic tells. Northwestern's 2024 guide to AI images (Kamali, Groh et al.) lists "artifacts, inconsistencies, and implausibilities that often appear in AI-generated images." Hands: "People in AI-generated images often have missing fingers, extra fingers, merged fingers, nonexistent fingernails, and unlikely hand proportions." Text: "AI-generated text can appear glyph-like, but be in a nonexistent language or produce nonexistent words, spelling errors, and incomprehensible sentences." Shadows: "AI-generated images can produce shadows cast in inconsistent directions or in shapes that do not correspond to their source."

But they're fading. The same guide: "Text in AI image generation is an area that is rapidly improving and these artifacts may not always be noticeable at first glance." NIST (Nov 2024): "…humans may find detection more difficult as synthetic content generation continues to increase in sophistication."

About half the time. In "As Good As A Coin Toss" (Cooke et al., CSIS, 2024), 1,276 people judged real and fake images, audio, video and audiovisual clips. The authors: "…on average, people struggled to distinguish between synthetic and authentic media, with the mean detection performance close to a chance level performance of 50%." That's the average across all those media types, not video alone.

So check the source, not the pixels. Tap the name and find the original upload. Northwestern's guide on reverse image search: "This may trace you back to the original source of the image…" and "Be sure to do some research on the image and the scene portrayed through reputable news sources or official social media accounts."

Then look for an AI label or Content Credentials. YouTube Help: "We require creators to disclose AI-generated or altered content that seems realistic." And "YouTube may automatically apply an AI label on the video player or in the expanded description for" content made with YouTube's own tools, "Content that contains C2PA metadata" and content its systems detect. TikTok (May 9, 2024): "Content Credentials attach metadata to content, which we can use to instantly recognize and label AIGC." contentcredentials.org: "The Content Credentials pin signals that the content contains information about its provenance."

No label? That proves nothing. NIST: "Metadata recorded within a file can similarly be stripped altogether, as it often is when files are shared (e.g., via social media platforms)." TikTok said its auto-labels "may be gradual at first, since it needs to have the Content Credentials metadata for us to identify and label it."

Do you check who posted it? Yes or no.

Fine print: every clip in this video is hand-drawn by us. None of it is AI footage or real footage, and no real person, account, app screen or logo is shown. The tells (a sixth finger, a misspelled sign, a shadow toward the sun) are our own drawings of the cues in Northwestern's guide, which is about AI images; we show them on a frame of our drawn clip. The clean redraw is made up too. The AI label and CR chips are our drawings, not real app labels. The 50% is the study's average over images, audio, video and audiovisual stimuli. "We all hunt for glitches" is our framing, not a survey result. A missing camera badge doesn't prove a video is fake either: YouTube's "Captured with a camera" page says "If it's missing, it doesn't mean the content has modified audio or visuals."

Sources (all read Nov 4, 2026):
- Kamali, Nakamura, Chatzimparmpas, Hullman and Groh (Northwestern University, Kellogg), "How to Distinguish AI-Generated Images from Authentic Photographs", arXiv 2406.08651, submitted Jun 12, 2024: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08651
- Cooke, Edwards, Barkoff and Kelly (CSIS), "As Good As A Coin Toss: Human detection of AI-generated images, videos, audio, and audiovisual stimuli", arXiv 2403.16760, v1 Mar 25, 2024: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16760
```
