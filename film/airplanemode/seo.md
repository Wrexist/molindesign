```
What does airplane mode really protect? Two things, under two agencies' rules. The FAA looks after the plane's radios and navigation; its 2013 panel found most airliners can tolerate phones, except in low visibility. The ban on cell signals in the air is the FCC's, from 1991, written to protect cell networks on the ground. In October 2026 the FCC voted to let drones use that band. Your phone's cell signal is still banned.

The plane. FAA Administrator Michael Huerta, remarks of Oct 31, 2013: "The committee determined that most commercial airplanes can tolerate radio interference from portable electronic devices." And: "But, the committee found that in some instances of low visibility – about one percent of flights – some landing systems may not be proven to tolerate the interference. In those cases, passengers should be asked to turn-off devices." If the crew asks, switch your phone off.

The towers. The FCC's rule, 47 CFR 22.925: "Cellular telephones installed in or carried aboard airplanes, balloons or any other type of aircraft must not be operated while such aircraft are airborne (not touching the ground)." The FCC on why (FCC 13-157, released Dec 13, 2013): "This prohibition was adopted in 1991 to guard against the threat of harmful interference from airborne use of cellular phones to terrestrial cellular networks. The Commission's prohibition was not to ensure interference-free operation of avionics equipment." Up high, a phone's "signal would be received by multiple terrestrial cell sites in a given market, causing harmful interference."

Why the FAA asks for it too. FAA Advisory Circular AC 91.21-1D (Oct 27, 2017): "The FAA supports this restriction on airborne cellular telephone use while in U.S. airspace." FAA InFO 13010 (Oct 28, 2013): "Requiring "Airplane Mode" during PED operation will help prevent violation of these regulations."

The drone vote. FCC 26-72 (adopted Oct 29, 2026, released Oct 30, 2026): "We, therefore, modify section 22.925 of the Commission's rules to allow 800 MHz Cellular spectrum to be used by unmanned aircraft and unmanned aircraft systems." And: "We make clear that this Report and Order does not affect the provision in section 22.925 that prohibits customers from operating their mobile devices while an aircraft is airborne."

Wi-Fi and Bluetooth. Huerta, Oct 31, 2013: "However, you will be able to connect through Wi-Fi to an airplane's wireless network if the airline provides the service. And you will be able to connect to Bluetooth accessories like a mouse or keyboard."

So, did you think it was for the plane? Yes or no.

Fine print: The FCC's drone rule was voted in October 2026 and was not yet in effect when we checked on Nov 11, 2026; it takes effect 30 days after it is published in the Federal Register. The cell ban is the FCC's, from 1991 (strictly the 800 MHz cellular band). Wi-Fi (if your airline offers it) and Bluetooth accessories are allowed per the FAA; each airline sets its own rules. "Most airliners can tolerate phones" is the FAA's 2013 panel finding, not a test of every plane or phone. This film makes no claim that phones would or wouldn't crash a plane.   **Reply:** Some have reported it. A UK CAA paper from April 2003 lists crew reports of "noise in the flight crew headphones" linked to phones, with older phones of that era. The same paper says "The difficulties experienced in trying to reproduce the events have led many (including pilots) to question whether a genuine problem exists." It's a UK study, so we kept it out of a US video.
```
- **If:** So drones can use cell service now?
  **Reply:** Not yet. The FCC voted on Oct 29, 2026 (FCC 26-72) to let uncrewed aircraft use the 800 MHz cellular band.
