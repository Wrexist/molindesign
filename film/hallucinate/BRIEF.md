# Hallucinate: why AI makes things up

Factory #3 · slug `hallucinate` · researched 2026-10-23 · follows `film/nextword/` (Next word) and `film/strawberry/` (Strawberry). Channel: Tech, explained (not named in the script). American English, US audience, no music, AI voice (Chatterbox), disclosed in the post.

## 1. Topic and one hard idea

**A chatbot is a guesser, and a guesser always has a next word: a fake court case is as easy to guess as a real one, and nothing makes it say "I don't know", because AI gets graded like a multiple-choice test where a blank scores zero and a guess might score.**

The real case carries the film: in *Mata v. Avianca* (federal court, Manhattan, 2023) ChatGPT invented six court cases. It even told the lawyer one of them was real and that it had double-checked. The lawyers were fined. The second half explains why, in two steps: guessing (one line linking back to Next word, with no tokens recap) and grading (the multiple-choice test and the old SAT's quarter-point penalty). It closes on OpenAI's own numbers: a model that skips when unsure is wrong far less often.

**Main search phrase: `AI hallucination`**. It's spoken at about 4.5 s (line `name`), it should be on screen in the first 2 s, and it goes in the caption's first line. Secondary phrases for the SEO agent: `why does AI make things up` (spoken, line `why`), `ChatGPT fake cases`, `ChatGPT lawyer`. I couldn't check search volumes: the Semrush connector returned "no API units", so the choice is a judgment call. "AI hallucination" is the name people know, and the other two are how people search for this news story.

**DM-able moment:** lines `asked` + `yes`. The lawyer asked ChatGPT, "Is Varghese a real case?" It said yes. When he pushed, it said it had double-checked and that the case was on Westlaw and LexisNexis. The screenshots are in the court record (Appendix B of the sanctions opinion), so the film can show the real wording. A runner-up is the old SAT's quarter-point penalty, which every American parent remembers.

## 2. Hook options

1. **"This court case doesn't exist. ChatGPT made it up."** (chosen)
2. "This court case doesn't exist. A lawyer still cited it. ChatGPT invented it." (the backlog hook)
3. "A lawyer asked ChatGPT, is this case real? It said yes."

**Why 1:** it's the backlog hook made shorter and more accurate.
- **"Still" was wrong.** The backlog hook's "a lawyer still cited it" implies he knew the case was fake and cited it anyway. The court did find bad faith later, but that was for standing by the cases after they were questioned. When he first filed, he hadn't checked them (sanctions opinion, ¶¶ 11–12).
- **It's shorter.** Three sentences become two, and 14 words become 9.
- **It's silent-friendly.** Frame 0 shows a real-looking case caption with "DOESN'T EXIST" stamped on it, which reads with the sound off.
- **It loops.** The last line, "Even when the answer doesn't exist.", runs straight back into "This court case doesn't exist."

Option 3 is the best story beat, so it's kept as the DM-able moment at 21–28 s. As an opener it would give the payoff away.

**Kept out of the narration on purpose**
- **Why ChatGPT kept saying "real" when asked.** The film's explanation is the general one: a guesser always has a next word, and "Yes, it's real" is the likeliest next words after "Is this real?". Nothing in the record says what happened inside that particular chat, so the voice never claims a specific mechanism for it.
- **The bad-faith finding.** The court sanctioned the lawyers for standing by the cases after they were questioned (and for a false "on vacation" letter), not for using ChatGPT. The judge wrote that "there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable artificial intelligence tool". The film shouldn't imply the fine was for using AI. "And fined the lawyers $5,000" is neutral; keep any screen text neutral too.
- **Who exactly did what.** Schwartz wrote the filing, and LoDuca signed it and filed it under his name. "A lawyer filed it" and "a lawyer asks ChatGPT" are both true of the pair, so I didn't name them in the voice. Names on screen are fine and come from the public record.
- **"OpenAI fixed it."** OpenAI argues the fix is changing how tests are graded. The 75% vs 26% gap compares two different models (o4-mini and gpt-5-thinking-mini), not one model before and after. The script says "a model that rarely skipped" and "one that skips", which is accurate. Don't caption it as "after the fix".
- **The tracker number as "AI's fault in 2,000 cases".** The database includes some cases where AI use was only alleged. "Court cases like this" is the safe wording; never say "2,000 lawyers fined".
- **Whether today's chatbots still invent cases.** The tracker says courts are still finding them in 2026. I didn't test live chatbots.

## 5. Open questions for the director

1. **Pronunciation of "Varghese".** The family name is said like "var-GEEZ". `say` spells it "Var-geese"; listen and re-roll with `take`. It's in `names` for the Whisper check.
2. **Length.** 21 lines, 216 spoken words. The chained `at` estimates put the last word near 85 s, with `dur` at 86.7 s. Chatterbox reads fast, so the real cut will likely land around 72–78 s. If it runs long, cut `catch` first (3.6 s; `button` → `test` still flows), then `learn`.
3. **Showing the screenshots.** The Appendix B screenshots are court records (public). I'd recreate them in house type with the exact wording rather than paste the scanned images, and label them "from the court record". Don't use OpenAI's logo.
4. **Real names on screen.** Lawyers' and the judge's names are public record. Use them only as small attribution labels (the judge's quote card), not as the faces of the story. Keep the plaintiff to "Mata v. Avianca".
5. **The `tested`/`skip` pair.** These compare two models, not a before and after. Keep the "right answers: 24% vs 22%" small print, or the jury may flag the numbers as cherry-picked.
6. **Pinned-comment idea for the SEO agent:** "The fake case even named three real judges, and one of them sits on a different court." (fact 6)
